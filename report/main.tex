\begin{abstract}
A bank needs trusted answers to three kinds of questions: how money flows in and out over
time, what happened on a given account, and whether the data and the pipeline behind those
answers can be trusted. This report builds and evaluates a data engineering solution for
these questions on the public Berka (PKDD'99) dataset of a Czech bank: 1,056,320
transactions on 4,500 accounts from 1993 to 1998, extended with a simulated membership tier
whose history must be preserved. We propose a Kimball dimensional data warehouse with one
transaction fact and five dimensions, including a Type~2 slowly changing customer dimension.
Data is ingested in batches through a Parquet data lake, processed with Apache Spark,
stored in ClickHouse, and controlled through an operational store in PostgreSQL that keeps
pipeline runs, rejected records, fraud alerts, their handling, and backups. One application
with three tabs serves three user groups and eight business requirements. The solution is
assessed on data correctness, performance, and effectiveness of data exploitation through
eight scripted evaluation scenarios, each reconciled with the source, and four benchmarks
that each change one factor: data model, warehouse technology, architecture of the
operational store, and pipeline parallelism. All eight scenarios pass (38 of 38 checks);
cash-flow totals match the source to the cent. Against the same star schema in PostgreSQL,
ClickHouse answers aggregation queries 3.3--22 times faster in 8.7 times less space, while
PostgreSQL is faster for single-account lookups that ClickHouse still answers in under
20~ms. A One Big Table is 1.5--3.4 times faster than the star schema but must rewrite
150,058 rows to correct one district name. Keeping the operational data in PostgreSQL is
8--70 times faster than in ClickHouse and enforces five integrity guarantees that
ClickHouse does not.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Retail banking is one of the most data-intensive industries. Every deposit, payment,
withdrawal, and transfer produces a record which, once aggregated, shows how money flows
through the bank, how each account behaves, and where risk emerges. Business managers,
analysts, and data engineers all depend on these records, but they ask different questions
of them and need different guarantees.

Operational transaction logs do not answer these questions on their own. They are
optimized for recording events, not for analysis; their codes are terse and partly
missing; customer attributes change over time without keeping their history; and nothing
in a raw log tells a user whether the latest load succeeded, which records were rejected,
or whether an earlier report still holds after a change. Data engineering closes this gap
by building a pipeline that ingests, validates, integrates, and models the data, and by
making the pipeline itself observable and recoverable.

This report is the technical report of the project \emph{Build a system where your data
engineering solution is proved successful (in a specific application domain)} for the Data
Engineering course (CO5173). The application domain is \textbf{banking} and the data
engineering solution is a \textbf{Kimball dimensional data warehouse} \cite{kimball2013}.
The objective is to show, with measurable evidence, that the solution turns raw bank
transactions into data that is correct, fast to query, and useful to the bank's users.

The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a star schema for bank transactions with a junk transaction-profile dimension, a
        role-playing district dimension, and a Type~2 slowly changing customer dimension
        that keeps the history of a simulated membership tier;
  \item a batch pipeline (data lake $\rightarrow$ Spark $\rightarrow$ ClickHouse) that
        validates every batch, isolates defective records instead of failing the batch,
        can re-run any batch without duplicates, and records every run in an operational
        store;
  \item an application with three tabs for three user groups that not only reads the
        warehouse but also handles alerts, runs scenario batches, and backs up and restores
        the warehouse;
  \item an evaluation through eight scenarios, one per business requirement, each
        reconciled with the source, and a benchmark against the same model in a row store.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the report is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:problem} defines the
problem: users, business requirements, and the data source. Section~\ref{sec:solutions}
presents the proposed solution and the alternatives. Section~\ref{sec:criteria} defines the
assessment criteria, Section~\ref{sec:method} the research methodology, and
Section~\ref{sec:analysis} analyzes the results. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Problem Statement}
The project uses the Berka dataset \cite{berka1999}, published for the PKDD'99 Discovery
Challenge: the anonymized records of a Czech bank. The data is real-world in origin but has
three properties that prevent its direct use. Its transaction classification is coded in
Czech and partly missing (Section~\ref{sec:data-quality}); it has no notion of a customer
attribute that changes over time; and as a set of static files it says nothing about how
new data arrives, fails, or is corrected. The problem addressed in this report is therefore:

\begin{quote}
\emph{How can a bank's transaction data be engineered into a correct, performant, and
exploitable analytical data warehouse whose loads are controlled, whose history is
preserved, and which demonstrably supports the business requirements of several user
groups?}
\end{quote}

\subsection{Data Characteristics}
\label{sec:data-characteristics}

\textbf{Volume and growth.} The 1,056,320 transactions grow every year, from 28,205 in
1993 to 322,277 in 1998, as the bank opened accounts. Each account has between 9 and 675
transactions (median 208), giving a long history per account, which BR02, BR03, and BR08
need.

\textbf{Cash-flow direction.} The \tbl{type} code gives the direction of money
(Table~\ref{tab:types}): \texttt{PRIJEM} (credit) is inflow, and \texttt{VYDAJ} (debit) and
\texttt{VYBER} (withdrawal) are outflow. Inflow totals 3,227,472,424.50~CZK and outflow
3,030,321,135.80~CZK. Amounts are never negative: 14 are zero, the mean is 5,924~CZK, the
median 2,100~CZK, the 99th percentile 45,455~CZK, and the maximum 87,400~CZK. The balance
after the transaction is negative in 2,999 records.

\subsection{Simulated Membership Tier}
\label{sec:tier}
BR01 and BR08 need an attribute that changes over time. A deterministic script assigns each
of the 4,500 owners an initial tier by the inflow of the first year after the account was
opened: Standard (below 150,000~CZK, 2,075 owners), Silver (to 300,000, 1,438), Gold (to
550,000, 698), and Platinum (289), effective from the account opening date. A year-end
re-evaluation with the same thresholds on the 1998 inflow, effective 1999-01-01, changes
the tier of 618 owners (13.7\%); together with their January 1999 transactions it forms
the KB08 scenario.

\subsection{Scope and Constraints}
The solution processes data in daily or monthly batches, not as a stream. End-of-period
balances and internal transfers between accounts of the bank are out of scope:
\tbl{balance\_after} is semi-additive and is shown only in account lookups. The application
has no access control (all three tabs are open), and PostgreSQL is outside the backup scope
of BR07. Finally, the solution must run on a single machine so that it can be demonstrated
and reproduced.

\section{Proposed Solutions}
\label{sec:solutions}

This section presents the proposed solution---a Kimball star schema on ClickHouse with an
operational store in PostgreSQL, fed by batches through a data lake and Spark---and the
alternatives against which it is assessed: another data model (One Big Table), another
warehouse technology (PostgreSQL), and another architecture for the operational store.

\subsection{First Solution: Kimball Star Schema on ClickHouse (Proposed)}
\label{sec:sol-kimball}

\subsubsection{Architecture}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the container-level architecture, drawn following the C4 model
\cite{c4model}. It has six blocks, all running in Docker Compose on one machine:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[A.] \textbf{Source.} The Berka files, the tier file, and scenario files in the same
        format.
  \item[B.] \textbf{Data lake.} Each batch is first copied, unchanged, as Parquet under
        \texttt{lake/batches/<batch\_id>/}. A batch can therefore be traced and re-run from
        exactly the input it had, even after the source changes.
  \item[C.] \textbf{Pipeline.} PySpark jobs read one batch, validate it, standardize codes,
        maintain the dimensions (including SCD2), load the fact, reconcile the batch with
        its input, and evaluate the alert rules on the warehouse. Every run and step is
        written to block E.
  \item[D.] \textbf{Data warehouse.} ClickHouse holds the star schema (database
        \texttt{dw}) and a separate backup disk.
  \item[E.] \textbf{Operational store.} PostgreSQL holds what the bank must be able to
        update and audit: batches, runs, steps, rejected records, alert rules, alerts and
        their handling, and the backup catalog.
  \item[F.] \textbf{Application.} One Streamlit application with three tabs reads D and E,
        writes alert handling to E, and sends allow-listed commands to C (run a scenario,
        re-run a batch, back up, restore) through a small runner service.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Dimensional Model}
The model follows Kimball's four-step design process \cite{kimball2013}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Business process:} account transactions.
  \item \textbf{Grain:} one fact row per transaction (atomic grain), so that every BR,
        including the transaction list of BR02 and the evidence of BR03, is answered from
        the same table.
  \item \textbf{Dimensions} (Fig.~\ref{fig:star}):
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \tbl{dim\_date}: a calendar with year, quarter, month, \tbl{year\_month}, day of
          week, and weekend flag.
    \item \tbl{dim\_account} (Type~1): account, opening date, statement frequency, and its
          branch district.
    \item \tbl{dim\_customer} (\textbf{Type~2}): the owner, with gender and birth date
          decoded from the birth number, residence district, and membership tier. Each
          tier is a version with its own surrogate key, \tbl{valid\_from} (inclusive),
          \tbl{valid\_to} (exclusive; 2299-12-31 for the current version), and
          \tbl{is\_current}.
    \item \tbl{dim\_transaction\_profile}: a \emph{junk dimension} of the 15 standardized
          combinations of type, operation, and purpose, with readable names and the
          cash-flow direction (IN/OUT).
    \item \tbl{dim\_district}: 77 districts in 8 regions, used in two roles---branch
          district through \tbl{dim\_account} and residence district through
          \tbl{dim\_customer} (an outrigger rather than a direct fact key).
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Facts:} \tbl{amount} is unsigned and \emph{additive}; inflow and outflow
        are obtained by splitting it on the direction of the profile, and net flow is
        their difference. \tbl{balance\_after} is \emph{semi-additive} and is never summed.
        \tbl{load\_batch\_id} and \tbl{loaded\_at} tie every row to the batch that loaded
        it.
\end{enumerate}
The key to BR08 is how the fact row chooses its customer version: the pipeline joins each
transaction to the owner version whose validity interval contains the transaction date.
A tier change therefore creates a new version for future transactions without touching the
fact rows of earlier periods.

\subsubsection{Batches and Re-Runs}
The history is loaded as one reference batch (\texttt{REF\_INITIAL}: districts, accounts,
owners, and initial tiers) followed by 72 monthly batches (\texttt{HIST\_1993\_01} to
\texttt{HIST\_1998\_12}). Each scenario is a further batch (e.g., \texttt{KB05\_DATA\_ERRORS}).
Every batch can be re-run from its data lake snapshot. A re-run skips transactions that
the same batch has already loaded and is recorded as a \texttt{RERUN}; the fact table is
additionally a \texttt{ReplacingMergeTree} keyed by transaction, so a re-run cannot create
duplicates.

\subsubsection{Technology for Data Processing}
\textbf{Apache Spark} \cite{zaharia2016spark} (PySpark, local mode) executes the pipeline.
Its DataFrame API expresses the validation rules declaratively, and the same jobs would
run on a cluster without change. Validation applies rules V1--V7
(Table~\ref{tab:vrules}); a failing record is written with its rule, reason, and original
content to \tbl{ops.rejected\_record}, and the rest of the batch continues. Listing~\ref{lst:scd}

if it were applied after the join, a transaction outside every version would silently
disappear, whereas here it keeps a null key and is rejected under V7.

Fraud alerts (BR03) are computed after each load from the warehouse with two rules whose
parameters live in \tbl{ops.alert\_rule}, so they can be tuned without changing code:
\textbf{R1} (``sharp increase in outflow'') fires when an account's outflow in a month
exceeds three times its average monthly outflow of the previous six months and 50,000~CZK;
\textbf{R2} (``dormant account reactivated'') fires on an outgoing transaction after at
least 180 days without any transaction. Each alert stores a readable reason, the figures
used (\tbl{evidence}), and the related transaction ids. Alerts are unique per account, rule,
and period, so re-running a batch adds none. On the historical data the rules raise 1,697
alerts (1,695 R1 and 2 R2).

\subsubsection{Technology for Data Management}
\textbf{ClickHouse} \cite{schulze2024clickhouse} is a column-oriented analytical database.
Column storage reads only the columns a query touches and compresses them well, the
classic advantage of column stores for analytical workloads
\cite{stonebraker2005cstore,abadi2008column}. ClickHouse has no in-place update and no
enforced keys, which a Kimball design normally relies on. The solution handles this as
follows: dimensions are \texttt{ReplacingMergeTree} tables in which a Type~1 correction or
the closing of an SCD2 version is written as a new row with a later \tbl{updated\_at} and
read with \texttt{FINAL}; keys are assigned and checked by the pipeline; and the fact is
ordered by \texttt{(account\_sk, date\_key, transaction\_id)} and partitioned by year,
which matches the filters of BR01 (period) and BR02 (account and period)
(Listing~\ref{lst:ddl}). Backups use the native \texttt{BACKUP DATABASE \ldots\ TO Disk}
and \texttt{RESTORE} statements on a dedicated disk.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=SQL, caption={ClickHouse definition of the transaction fact table (from \texttt{dw.sql}).}, label={lst:ddl}]
CREATE TABLE IF NOT EXISTS dw.fact_transaction (
  transaction_id          UInt64,
  date_key                UInt32,  -- -> dim_date
  account_sk              UInt32,  -- -> dim_account
  customer_sk             UInt32,  -- owner version valid on the date
  transaction_profile_sk  UInt16,  -- -> dim_transaction_profile
  amount                  Decimal(18, 2),
  balance_after           Decimal(18, 2),
  counterparty_bank_code  Nullable(String),
  counterparty_account_ref Nullable(String),
  load_batch_id           LowCardinality(String),
  loaded_at               DateTime64(3)
) ENGINE = ReplacingMergeTree(loaded_at)
PARTITION BY intDiv(date_key, 10000)      -- one partition per year
ORDER BY (account_sk, date_key, transaction_id);
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{PostgreSQL} serves as the operational store. Unlike the warehouse, this data is
small, is updated in place, and must obey real constraints: primary and foreign keys,
\texttt{CHECK} constraints on every status, and a function that only allows valid alert
transitions (\emph{new} $\rightarrow$ \emph{in review} $\rightarrow$ \emph{resolved},
\emph{new} $\rightarrow$ \emph{resolved}, and reopening a resolved alert) and writes every
change with its note to \tbl{ops.alert\_action}. Keeping this data out of the warehouse
means that analysts' notes are never mixed with bank figures, and that a warehouse restore
never loses the record of what happened.

\subsubsection{Big Data Considerations}
The course asks for the solution to be assessed in a Big Data context. We discuss it along
the common ``V'' characteristics.

\textbf{Volume.} Berka holds about one million transactions, which a single machine
handles easily. The design scales without a change to the model: Spark distributes the
same jobs over a cluster, and ClickHouse can shard the fact table with its
\texttt{Distributed} engine. The fact table is 18.5~MB compressed for 1.06 million rows in
ClickHouse.

\textbf{Velocity.} The bank produced at most 322,277 transactions a year, under 1,000 a
day, which suits daily batches. A monthly batch of about 15,000 transactions is processed
in a median of 3.4~s, so the pipeline has ample headroom.

\textbf{Variety.} The data is structured. Spark reads every file with an explicit schema,
and the data lake stores it with the data.

\textbf{Veracity.} Veracity is addressed by validation on every batch, isolation of failing
records with their reasons, reconciliation of each batch with its input, and a full
reconciliation with the source (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).

\textbf{Value.} Value is delivered through the eight BRs, each tied to a business
objective (Table~\ref{tab:objectives}) and to an application view
(Table~\ref{tab:appmap}).

\subsection{Second Solution: One Big Table on ClickHouse}
\label{sec:sol-obt}
The second solution keeps the same pipeline and technology but replaces the star schema
with a single denormalized table: every fact row carries all dimension attributes,
including both district roles and the tier with its validity interval. This design is
often recommended for column stores because it removes joins at query time, and it keeps
SCD2 semantics as of the transaction date because the tier is copied when the row is
loaded. Its costs are redundancy and expensive corrections: changing one Type~1 attribute
requires rewriting every fact row that carries it, which ClickHouse does with an
asynchronous mutation.

\subsection{Third Solution: Star Schema on PostgreSQL (Row Store)}
\label{sec:sol-postgres}
The third solution keeps the same pipeline, star schema, and rows, but stores the
warehouse in PostgreSQL, a row-oriented relational database (decision Q-K of the team). It
offers full support for the Kimball design---enforced primary and foreign keys,
transactions, and in-place updates---and B-tree indexes for point lookups, but reads whole
rows even when a query needs only a few columns. To make the comparison fair, it is set up
as in the Phase~1 benchmark: primary and foreign keys and indexes on the fact's filter and
join columns (\tbl{date\_key}; \tbl{account\_sk}, \tbl{date\_key}; \tbl{customer\_sk}) and
on \tbl{dim\_customer.client\_id}, created after the bulk load; up-to-date statistics; and
256~MB of shared buffers, 64~MB of work memory, and up to four parallel workers.

\subsection{Alternative Architecture: Operational Data in ClickHouse}
\label{sec:sol-ops}
The proposed architecture adds a second database (block E). The alternative removes it
and keeps runs, alerts, and their handling in ClickHouse next to the warehouse. Because
ClickHouse has no in-place update, a status change is either written as a new row version
of a \texttt{ReplacingMergeTree} table and read with \texttt{FINAL}, or applied as an
\texttt{ALTER TABLE \ldots\ UPDATE} mutation; the validity of a transition must be checked
by the application.

\subsection{Related Work}
Kimball's bottom-up dimensional modeling \cite{kimball2013} contrasts with Inmon's top-down
approach \cite{inmon2005}, in which a normalized enterprise data warehouse feeds
departmental marts, and with Data Vault \cite{linstedt2015}, which separates business keys,
relationships, and descriptive attributes to favor auditability and change. For a single
business process and a small team, Kimball offers the shortest path from data to
user-facing queries, and its slowly changing dimensions cover the history requirement of
BR08. The lakehouse paradigm \cite{armbrust2021lakehouse} unifies data lakes and warehouses
on open formats. The advantages of column stores for analytical workloads are well
established \cite{stonebraker2005cstore,abadi2008column}, and the Star Schema Benchmark
\cite{oneil2009ssb} adapts TPC-H to star schemas. The Berka dataset has been widely used
for loan-default prediction and customer analysis since the PKDD'99 challenge
\cite{berka1999}; here it is used as the source of a warehouse rather than of a model.

\section{Criteria for Assessing Solutions}

The solutions are assessed against the three criteria required by the course. Each
criterion is broken down into measurable metrics with explicit targets, defined before
measurement. The eight evaluation scenarios KB01--KB08 (Table~\ref{tab:brs}) are the

load, and a reconciliation with the source.

\subsection{Criterion 1: Data Correctness}
Correctness metrics follow the ISO/IEC 25012 characteristics \cite{iso25012}
(Table~\ref{tab:correctness-criteria}). Every result is checked against something
independent of the warehouse: the source files, recomputed by a separate program; the

\subsection{Criterion 2: Performance}
Performance is assessed by: (i) the latency of each BR query, as the median and 95th
percentile of warm runs plus the cold run; (ii) throughput and latency with 1, 4, and 8
concurrent users; (iii) storage size and load time; (iv) the cost of correcting a Type~1
attribute; (v) the latency of the operational workload (alert handling, run logging) and
the integrity guarantees the store enforces by itself; and (vi) pipeline time per batch
and per step, and its scaling with CPU cores. A solution is considered better on a metric
only if it does not violate Criterion~1.

\subsection{Criterion 3: Effectiveness of Data Exploitation}
Effectiveness is assessed per BR by (i) whether the BR is demonstrated end to end through
the application, (ii) the number of user interactions needed to obtain the answer, and
(iii) the time to answer.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1.} ClickHouse answers the aggregation queries of BR01, BR03, and BR08
        faster than PostgreSQL with the same schema, and stores the data in less space.
  \item \textbf{H2.} For single-account or single-client lookups (BR02, client versions of
        BR08), the B-tree indexes of PostgreSQL narrow or reverse the gap.
  \item \textbf{H3.} The time of a batch is dominated by fixed overhead rather than by the
        number of transactions in it.
  \item \textbf{H4.} The One Big Table answers queries slightly faster than the star
        schema, but needs more space and makes Type~1 corrections much more expensive.
  \item \textbf{H5.} A separate relational operational store is faster for the
        operational workload and enforces guarantees that the column store cannot.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Scenarios}
The historical data was loaded first (one reference batch and 72 monthly batches). The
scenario files were then generated by a deterministic script, together with their
expected results, before any of them was loaded:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{KB03:} five transactions built to trigger the alert rules: three accounts
        withdraw 60,000~CZK in February 1999 (R1) and two accounts withdraw after 196 days
        without activity (R2).
  \item \textbf{KB05:} 20 transactions, of which 10 are valid and 10 each violate one rule

  \item \textbf{KB07:} the warehouse is backed up, 20 deposits are loaded, and the
        warehouse is restored.
  \item \textbf{KB08:} 618 tier changes effective 1999-01-01 and 1,236 transactions of
        January 1999 for those owners.
\end{itemize}
KB01, KB02, KB04, and KB06 use the loaded data, the alerts of KB03, and the runs that
already exist (including one run that genuinely failed during development).

\subsection{Data Correctness Checks}
An evaluation program recomputes the source independently: it reads the Berka file and
the scenario files with pandas, assigns each transaction its owner from \tbl{disp} and its
tier from the tier files by date, and compares the result with the warehouse in integer
cents. It then compares every scenario with its expected result and the operational store,
and runs the data quality checks of Table~\ref{tab:correctness-criteria}. KB04 is executed
inside a database transaction that is rolled back, so the evaluation leaves no trace. The
program only reads the warehouse.

\subsection{Benchmark Design}
The benchmark followed the TPC methodology \cite{tpch,tpcds} with the actual queries of
the application as workload. Four comparisons were made, each changing one factor
(Table~\ref{tab:bench}). The three warehouse solutions were loaded from the same CSV export
of the warehouse and hold the same 1,057,571 fact rows; every write test ran on these
copies, never on the warehouse or the operational store. Nine queries cover BR01 (four),
BR02 (two), BR03 (rule R1 over the whole history, whose result, 1,698 account-months,
equals the number of R1 alerts), and BR08 (two). Each query was run six times per solution:
the first (cold) run was recorded separately, with the ClickHouse caches dropped before
it, and the median and 95th percentile of the remaining five (warm) runs were reported.
Times were measured by the client and include the transfer of the result, and the result
sets were compared row by row with those of the proposed solution. For concurrency, 1, 4,
or 8 clients repeated a mix of one query per user group (BR01 by tier, BR02 for a random
account, BR08 by tier) for 15~s. The operational workload (B3) was executed one call at a
time, as the application and the pipeline do: 200 status changes, 200 logged steps, and
50 reads of each kind. For B4, every configuration ran in a new Spark JVM, and the
session start-up time was reported separately. Only the original data ($\times$1) was
measured.

\textbf{Warehouse technology (B2).} The results appear to support H1. On the six
aggregation queries ClickHouse was 3.3 to 22 times faster, and the gap grew with the work
per query: it was smallest for a single sum over one year and largest when the query also
joined the customer dimension and counted distinct clients (BR01 by tier, BR08) or applied
a window function per account (BR03). ClickHouse stored the data in 8.7 times less space
(Table~\ref{tab:storage}) and, with eight concurrent users, served 93 queries per second
against 12 for PostgreSQL, whose p95 latency rose to almost 2~s
(Table~\ref{tab:concurrency}). H2 is supported more strongly than expected: for the three
lookups of one account or one client, PostgreSQL was 17 to 46 times faster, answering in
about 1~ms through its indexes. In ClickHouse these queries cost 6--18~ms, which seems to
be mostly fixed cost---reading the small dimensions with \texttt{FINAL} and building the
hash tables of the joins---since the sort key already restricts the fact to one account.

\textbf{Data model (B1).} H4 appears to hold. The One Big Table was 1.5 to 3.4 times
faster than the star schema, but the absolute difference was only 3--16~ms, and it needed
1.37 times more space (twice as much before compression). Its weakness is change: renaming
one district rewrote 150,058 fact rows in 99~ms instead of one dimension row, and
correcting one client's birth date rewrote 330 rows instead of two. These numbers grow
with the data, whereas the star schema's do not.

\textbf{Operational store (B3).} H5 appears to hold (Table~\ref{tab:ops}). PostgreSQL was
8 to 70 times faster on every call the application and the pipeline make, because each
call touches one row, the workload a row store with indexes is built for. In ClickHouse,
400 small inserts created 400 data parts, the write pattern ClickHouse discourages, and a
mutation cannot change the version column of a \texttt{ReplacingMergeTree}. More
importantly, PostgreSQL enforced all five guarantees that BR03 and BR04 rely on, while
ClickHouse accepted every violation: an invalid status, an invalid transition, a duplicate
alert, history for a missing alert, and a status change whose history record failed.

\textbf{Pipeline (B4).} H3 appears to hold (Table~\ref{tab:pipeline}). Transforming the
whole history in one batch took 5.4--7.7~s, and adding cores beyond two barely helped,
because at about one million rows the job is dominated by fixed costs (planning, task
scheduling, small files). Splitting the same work into 72 monthly batches took 4 to 6
times longer for the same reason. The real pipeline needs a median of 3.4~s per monthly
batch, of which the fact load (1.42~s) and validation (0.85~s) are the largest steps.

\subsection{Interpretation}
The results suggest that the proposed solution meets the three criteria. On correctness,
the warehouse reproduces the source exactly, and the operational features---isolation of
defective records, idempotent re-runs, alerts without duplicates, restore, and history
preservation---behaved as specified in every scenario. On performance, the column store
appears to be the right choice for the workload that defines a warehouse, the aggregations
of U01 and U03, where it is up to 22 times faster, 8.7 times smaller, and sustains many
more concurrent users. The One Big Table is faster still, but by milliseconds, at the price
of rewriting large parts of the fact table for every correction. Each of the two
alternatives is better at one thing---PostgreSQL at point lookups and single-row updates,
the One Big Table at join-free scans---and the proposed architecture uses the first of
these where it matters: the operational store, whose workload consists of small lookups,
in-place updates, and constraints, is kept in PostgreSQL.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
The results should be interpreted with caution. All systems ran on the same virtual
machine as the client, and PostgreSQL used the moderate settings of the Phase~1
benchmark; partitioning, BRIN indexes, or materialized views could narrow its gap on
aggregations. The operating system's page cache could not be dropped from a container, so
``cold'' runs are only partly cold. Only the original data ($\times$1, about one million
rows) was measured: at this size Spark does not benefit from more cores, and the
advantage of the column store on aggregations is expected to grow with volume, but neither
trend was measured. The membership tier and the scenario data are simulated, so the tier figures
and alert counts describe the method rather than real customer behavior. Finally, the
interaction counts were taken along the user flow and not from user sessions.

\section{Conclusions and Recommendations}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This report presented a Kimball dimensional data warehouse for banking cash-flow analytics,
built on the Berka dataset with Spark, ClickHouse, and PostgreSQL. The pipeline loads data
in batches through a data lake, isolates defective records instead of failing, can re-run
any batch without duplicates, and records every run; a Type~2 customer dimension keeps the
history of a simulated membership tier; and one application with three tabs serves three
user groups and eight business requirements, including alert handling and the control of
the pipeline.

On data correctness, all eight evaluation scenarios passed (38 of 38 checks), and cash-flow
figures equal the source to the cent. On performance, ClickHouse answered aggregation
queries 3.3--22 times faster than the same model in PostgreSQL, in 8.7 times less space and
with eight times the throughput under concurrent use, while PostgreSQL was faster on
single-account lookups that ClickHouse still answers in under 20~ms. The One Big Table
saved a few milliseconds per query but made corrections rewrite up to 150,058 rows, and
the operational store was 8--70 times faster and enforced five integrity guarantees in
PostgreSQL that ClickHouse did not. On effectiveness, every BR was demonstrated through the
application in one to about seven interactions.

\textbf{Recommendations.} For a bank with this workload, we recommend the proposed
combination: a column store for the warehouse, because its users mainly aggregate, and a
relational database for the operational data, because it is updated in place and must
enforce constraints. Account lookups, if they become the dominant workload, can be served
from the row store or from a projection sorted by account in ClickHouse.

\textbf{Limitations.} The data ends in 1998 and has about one million transactions; the
membership tier and the scenarios are simulated; end-of-period balances and internal
transfers are out of scope; the application has no access control; and the operational
store is not part of the backup.

\textbf{Future work.} Add access control per user group; extend the model with loans and
standing orders as further business processes sharing conformed dimensions; measure the
solution at larger data volumes ($\times$10, $\times$50) and on a cluster; and replace the rule-based alerts with
learned anomaly detection evaluated against the same scenarios.
