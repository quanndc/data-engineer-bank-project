\begin{abstract}
Banks generate large volumes of transactional data, yet raw transaction logs rarely support
business decisions directly: they contain inconsistent identifiers, invalid attributes, and
no structure for analytical queries. This report addresses this problem for a synthetic
banking dataset of 1.42 million transactions and 1.05 million customer records. A profiling
study revealed critical quality defects, including 142,360 customer identifiers shared by
different individuals, 174,242 invalid dates of birth, and pre-built marts computed against
the wrong reference date. We propose a Kimball dimensional data warehouse in which
transactions are ingested through Apache Kafka, cleaned and conformed with Apache Spark
(batch and Structured Streaming), and stored as a star schema in ClickHouse, a
column-oriented database. Ten business requirements from seven user groups are served
through Apache Superset dashboards and a near-real-time Streamlit alerting page. The
proposed solution is assessed against alternative solutions---a One Big Table design, a
row-store warehouse in PostgreSQL, and an in-database streaming approach---on three
criteria: data correctness (based on ISO/IEC 25012), performance (a TPC-style benchmark at
scale factors $\times$1, $\times$10, and $\times$50), and effectiveness of data
exploitation through the application.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Retail banking is one of the most data-intensive industries. Every card purchase, transfer,
bill payment, and loan repayment produces a record that, once aggregated, reveals how
customers behave, which regions grow, which products are used, and where risk emerges.
Decision makers across a bank---from the executive board to the marketing, risk, and wealth
management teams---need these insights in a form that is correct, consistent, and fast to
query.

Operational transaction logs do not satisfy these needs on their own. They are optimized for
recording events rather than for analysis; customer attributes are often duplicated or
invalid; and each team tends to compute its own metrics from raw data, producing
conflicting numbers. Data engineering closes this gap by building a pipeline that ingests,
cleans, integrates, and models the data so that it can be exploited reliably.

This report is the technical report of the project \emph{Build a system where your data
engineering solution is proved successful (in a specific application domain)} for the Data
Engineering course (CO5173). The application domain is \textbf{banking} and the data
engineering solution is a \textbf{Kimball dimensional data warehouse} \cite{kimball2013}.
The objective is to show, with measurable evidence, that the solution turns a defective
banking dataset into data that is correct, performant, and useful to the bank's users.

The main contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a profiling study of the source dataset that identifies and quantifies its quality
        defects, organized by the ISO/IEC 25012 data quality model \cite{iso25012};
  \item a Kimball star schema for banking transactions, including a corrected customer
        identity model, a periodic balance snapshot for the semi-additive balance measure,
        and a streaming alert fact table;
  \item an end-to-end pipeline (Kafka $\rightarrow$ Spark $\rightarrow$ ClickHouse) with
        automated data quality checks, and a data-driven application covering ten business
        requirements for seven user groups;
  \item a systematic, TPC-style assessment \cite{tpch,tpcds} of the proposed solution
        against alternative solutions at three data scales.
\end{itemize}

The rest of the report is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:problem} defines the
problem: stakeholders, business requirements, and the characteristics and defects of the
data source. Section~\ref{sec:solutions} presents the proposed solution and the
alternatives. Section~\ref{sec:criteria} defines the criteria used to assess them.
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the research methodology. Section~\ref{sec:analysis}
analyzes and interprets the results, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} gives conclusions and
recommendations.

\begin{quote}
\emph{How can a banking transaction dataset with known quality defects be engineered into a
correct, performant, and exploitable analytical data warehouse that demonstrably supports
the business requirements of multiple user groups?}

\subsection{Data Characteristics}
\label{sec:data-characteristics}

\textbf{Volume and value.} The fact table holds 1,418,764 transactions with a total value
of 2.23 billion INR. Transaction amounts are highly skewed: the mean is 1,572.39 INR, the
median 459.49 INR, the 99th percentile 20,000 INR, and the maximum 1,560,034.99 INR.
Recorded balances range from 0 to 115.0 million INR (median 16,792.18 INR). Daily volume
ranges from 3 to 36,798 transactions (mean 25,796).

\textbf{Temporal coverage.} Coverage is uneven: August has 31 days of data (884,932
transactions), September 22 days (528,845), and October only 2 days (4,987); from
2016-09-22 onwards no day exceeds 10,000 transactions. In practice the data is complete
only from 2016-08-01 to about 2016-09-21, which must be kept in mind when trends (BR1) and
recency (BR4) are interpreted.

\subsection{Data Quality Defects}
\label{sec:data-quality}
Table~\ref{tab:dq} summarizes the defects found, organized by the ISO/IEC 25012 data
quality characteristics \cite{iso25012}. Three findings are decisive.

\emph{Customer identifier collisions.} There are 884,265 distinct \tbl{customer\_id}
values but 1,046,782 customer keys. Of the 142,360 identifiers mapped to more than one key,
141,466 have records with different dates of birth and 60,185 with different genders;
together they cover 304,877 customer records (29.1\%).
These are different individuals sharing an identifier, not one customer whose attributes
change over time; a slowly changing dimension would therefore model them incorrectly.

\emph{Invalid dates of birth.} 56,782 records hold the placeholder 1800-01-01, 117,460
hold dates after the end of the observation period (up to 2075-12-31), and 3,385 are null.
The future dates are consistent with a two-digit-year parsing error (e.g., ``75''
$\rightarrow$ 2075).

\emph{Unreliable pre-built marts.} The datamart tables contain 1,268,599 rows for 884,265
identifiers (up to 20 rows per identifier). In the RFM mart, recency ranges from 3,484 to
3,565 days, i.e., it was computed against the processing date (around 2026) rather than the
end of the data (2016-10-21), and the segments ``At Risk'' and ``Potential Loyalists'' have
exactly the same size (303,215). In the behavior segmentation mart,
\tbl{activity\_segment} takes a single value and 99.3\% of rows are ``Low Value''.

\subsection{Scope and Constraints}
The BRs were chosen to be answerable with the available data. Because the data spans only
55 days and most customers have a single transaction, requirements that depend on long
customer histories---churn prediction, customer lifetime value, or recurring payment
detection---are out of scope. BR5 depends on the cleaning of dates of birth and gender, and
BR8 uses the balance recorded at transaction time instead of a true end-of-day balance.
Finally, the solution must run on a single machine so that it can be demonstrated and
reproduced.

\section{Proposed Solutions}
\label{sec:solutions}

This section presents the proposed solution---a Kimball star schema on ClickHouse, fed by
Kafka and Spark---and the alternative solutions against which it is assessed.

\subsection{First Solution: Kimball Star Schema on ClickHouse (Proposed)}
\label{sec:sol-kimball}

\subsubsection{Architecture}
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the container-level architecture, drawn following the C4 model
\cite{c4model}. All components run in Docker Compose on a single machine:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ingestion.} A Python producer replays the transaction CSV into a Kafka topic
        in date order at a configurable rate, emulating a live transaction feed. Only
        transactions flow through Kafka; reference data (customers and locations) changes
        rarely and is read directly from the source CSV files.
  \item \textbf{Batch processing.} Spark reads transactions from the Kafka topic and
        reference data from the CSV files, writes them as Parquet (raw layer), applies
        cleaning rules (staging layer), builds dimensions and facts with surrogate keys
        (core layer), and loads them into ClickHouse.
  \item \textbf{Stream processing.} Spark Structured Streaming consumes the Kafka topic,
        evaluates anomaly rules, and writes alerts to ClickHouse (BR7).
  \item \textbf{Data management.} ClickHouse stores the star schema, the data marts, the
        alerts, and the \tbl{audit} table.
  \item \textbf{Data quality.} SQL checks run after each load and write their results to
        the \tbl{audit} table (BR9).
  \item \textbf{Application.} Superset serves one dashboard per user group; Streamlit serves
        the near-real-time alert page.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Dimensional Model}
The model follows Kimball's four-step design process \cite{kimball2013}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Business process:} customer account transactions.
  \item \textbf{Grain:} one fact row per transaction (atomic grain), so that every BR can be
        answered without loss of detail.
  \item \textbf{Dimensions} (Fig.~\ref{fig:star}):
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \tbl{dim\_date}: a full calendar for 2015--2017 (instead of the 55-row source),
          with day of week, weekend flag, week, month, quarter, and Indian public holidays.
    \item \tbl{dim\_customer}: one row per individual. The natural key is the triple
          (\tbl{customer\_id}, original DOB, gender), which separates the colliding
          individuals. Cleaned DOB, age group, gender, descriptive attributes, data quality
          flags, and an ``Unknown'' member are added.
    \item \tbl{dim\_location}: standardized location names. The source holds 9,354
          free-text locations and no state information, so a curated seed table maps the
          54 largest cities to a canonical city and state (e.g., ``DELHI'', ``NEW DELHI'',
          and ``LAL HOSPITAL NEW DELHI'' $\rightarrow$ Delhi); these cover 77.6\% of the
          transactions, and all other locations are grouped as ``Other''.
    \item \tbl{dim\_transaction\_type}: the four transaction types, with a flag stating
          that the labels were rule-inferred.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Facts:} \tbl{transaction\_amount} is \emph{additive} along every dimension.
        \tbl{account\_balance} is \emph{semi-additive}: it must not be summed over time, only
        averaged or taken at the end of a period. A periodic snapshot fact
        \tbl{fact\_customer\_balance\_snapshot} therefore records the last known balance per
        customer at each month end (BR8). A daily grain over the three-year calendar
        would have produced about 1.1 billion rows; the monthly grain produces 2.8
        million and still answers BR8.
\end{enumerate}
Two further tables complete the model: \tbl{fact\_transaction\_alert}, written by the
streaming job (BR7), and \tbl{mart\_rfm}, an RFM mart \cite{hughes1994} rebuilt from the
fact table with 2016-10-21 as the reference date (BR4).

\textbf{Apache Spark} \cite{zaharia2016spark} performs the batch ETL. Its DataFrame API

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Customer cleaning and natural key construction in PySpark.}, label={lst:clean}]
from pyspark.sql import functions as F
REF = F.lit("2016-10-21").cast("date")
MIN_DOB = F.lit("1916-10-22").cast("date")

  # natural key on ORIGINAL attributes:
  # separates colliding individuals
  .withColumn("customer_nk", F.sha2(
      F.concat_ws("|", "customer_id",
          F.coalesce(dob.cast("string"),
                     F.lit("NA")),

\textbf{Spark Structured Streaming} \cite{armbrust2018structured} implements BR7 with the
same DataFrame API as the batch jobs (Listing~\ref{lst:stream}). A transaction raises a
\texttt{HIGH\_AMOUNT} alert when its amount exceeds $\tau = 20{,}000$~INR (the historical
99th percentile), and a \texttt{HIGH\_BALANCE\_RATIO} alert when it is at least 5,000~INR
and larger than the account balance. The rules were sized on the historical data: a
balance-ratio rule alone would flag 8--13\% of all transactions, because many balances
are small, so a minimum amount was added; together the two rules flag 1.82\%. Source keys
in the events are mapped to the conformed surrogate keys of \tbl{dim\_customer} and
\tbl{dim\_location} with a stream-static join, so alerts join the star schema directly.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Near-real-time anomaly detection with Spark Structured Streaming (BR7), simplified from \texttt{p4\_stream\_alerts.py}.}, label={lst:stream}]
events = (spark.readStream.format("kafka")
  .option("kafka.bootstrap.servers", BOOTSTRAP)
  .option("subscribe", "transactions")
  .load()
  .select(F.from_json(F.col("value")
      .cast("string"), TXN_SCHEMA).alias("t"))
  .select("t.*"))

amt = F.col("transaction_amount").cast(DEC)
bal = F.col("account_balance").cast(DEC)
alerts = (events
  .withColumn("rule",
      F.when(amt > 20000, "HIGH_AMOUNT")
       .when((amt >= 5000) & (amt > bal),
             "HIGH_BALANCE_RATIO"))
  .filter(F.col("rule").isNotNull())
  .withColumn("detected_at",
      F.current_timestamp())
  # source keys -> conformed surrogate keys
  .join(cust_map, "source_customer_key")
  .join(loc_map, "source_location_key"))

(alerts.writeStream
  .foreachBatch(write_batch)  # -> ClickHouse
  .option("checkpointLocation", CHECKPOINT)
  .trigger(processingTime="2 seconds")
  .start())
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Technology for Data Management}
\textbf{ClickHouse} \cite{schulze2024clickhouse} is a column-oriented analytical database.
Column storage reads only the columns a query touches and compresses them well, which is
the classic advantage of column stores for analytical workloads
\cite{stonebraker2005cstore,abadi2008column}. Its MergeTree engine sorts data by a primary
key and skips irrelevant data blocks. Listing~\ref{lst:ddl} shows the fact table: monthly
partitions and ordering by date and location match the filters of BR1--BR3.

% NOTE: reference design -- replace with the final code from the repository before submission.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=SQL, caption={ClickHouse definition of the transaction fact table.}, label={lst:ddl}]
CREATE TABLE dwh.fact_transactions (
  transaction_key    UInt64,
  date_key           UInt32,
  customer_key       UInt64,
  location_key       UInt32,
  txn_type_key       UInt8,
  transaction_amount Decimal(18, 2),
  account_balance    Decimal(18, 2),
  dq_flags           UInt8
) ENGINE = MergeTree
-- one partition per month
PARTITION BY intDiv(date_key, 100)
ORDER BY (date_key, location_key, customer_key);
\end{lstlisting}

Listing~\ref{lst:rfm} rebuilds the RFM mart for BR4 with the correct reference date. Scores
use fixed thresholds instead of equal-size quantiles: since 70.8\% of customer records
have a single transaction, quantile-based scores would assign different scores to identical
customers---the same flaw found in the pre-built source mart.

% NOTE: reference design -- replace with the final code from the repository before submission.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=SQL, caption={RFM mart with the correct reference date and fixed score thresholds (BR4).}, label={lst:rfm}]
INSERT INTO mart.rfm
SELECT customer_key, recency, frequency, monetary,
  multiIf(recency <= 7, 5, recency <= 21, 4,
          recency <= 42, 3, recency <= 63, 2, 1)
                                   AS r_score,
  least(frequency, 5)              AS f_score,
  multiIf(monetary < 500, 1, monetary < 2000, 2,
          monetary < 10000, 3,
          monetary < 50000, 4, 5)  AS m_score
FROM (
  SELECT f.customer_key,
    dateDiff('day', max(d.full_date),
             toDate('2016-10-21')) AS recency,
    count()                        AS frequency,
    sum(f.transaction_amount)      AS monetary
  FROM dwh.fact_transactions AS f
  JOIN dwh.dim_date AS d
    ON f.date_key = d.date_key
  GROUP BY f.customer_key
);
\end{lstlisting}

% NOTE: reference design -- replace with the final code from the repository before submission.
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, caption={Auto-refreshing risk alert page in Streamlit (BR7).}, label={lst:streamlit}]
import clickhouse_connect
import streamlit as st

ch = clickhouse_connect.get_client(
    host="clickhouse")
rule = st.selectbox("Rule", ["ALL", "HIGH_AMOUNT",
                             "HIGH_BALANCE_RATIO"])

@st.fragment(run_every="2s")   # auto-refresh
def alert_feed():
    df = ch.query_df(
        "SELECT detected_at, customer_key, "
        "transaction_amount, rule "
        "FROM dwh.fact_transaction_alert "
        "ORDER BY detected_at DESC LIMIT 200")
    if rule != "ALL":
        df = df[df["rule"] == rule]
    st.metric("Recent alerts", len(df))
    st.dataframe(df)

alert_feed()
\end{lstlisting}

\subsubsection{Big Data Considerations}
The course asks for the solution to be assessed in a Big Data context. We discuss it along
the common ``V'' characteristics of Big Data.

\textbf{Volume.} The source holds 1.42 million transactions, which a single machine handles
easily. To test the solution at larger volumes, the data is replicated up to 70.9 million
transactions (Section~\ref{sec:method}). The design scales beyond one machine without
changing the model: Spark distributes the same jobs over a cluster, and ClickHouse can
split the fact table into shards with its \texttt{Distributed} table engine and copy them
with \texttt{ReplicatedMergeTree}. Within one node, column compression, monthly partitions,
and the sort key keep scans small.

\textbf{Velocity.} The busiest day in the source has 36,798 transactions, an average of
about 0.43 transactions per second, far below the capacity of the pipeline. The producer
therefore replays the data at a configurable rate to emulate bursts of thousands of events
per second. Kafka absorbs bursts and retains events for replay, its partitions allow
several consumers to read in parallel, and Structured Streaming processes the stream in
two-second micro-batches, which bounds the alert latency.

\textbf{Variety.} The data is structured: CSV files and JSON messages with a fixed schema.
Spark enforces explicit schemas when reading, and Parquet stores them with the data.
Semi-structured and unstructured sources (e.g., call-center notes or mobile app logs) are
outside the scope of this project.

\textbf{Veracity.} Veracity is the main Big Data challenge of this dataset
(Section~\ref{sec:data-quality}). The solution addresses it with cleaning rules in the
staging layer, data quality flags on dimension and fact rows, and automated checks recorded
in the \tbl{audit} table.

\textbf{Value.} Value is delivered through the ten BRs, each tied to a business objective
(Table~\ref{tab:objectives}) and to an application view (Table~\ref{tab:appmap}).

\subsection{Second Solution: One Big Table on ClickHouse}
\label{sec:sol-obt}
The second solution keeps the same pipeline and technologies but replaces the star schema
with a single denormalized table that joins every fact row with all dimension attributes,
stored in ClickHouse. This design is often recommended for column stores because it removes
joins at query time. Its costs are redundancy (customer and location attributes repeated in
every transaction row) and expensive updates: changing one customer attribute requires
rewriting every fact row of that customer instead of one dimension row.

\subsection{Third Solution: Star Schema on PostgreSQL (Row Store)}
\label{sec:sol-postgres}
The third solution keeps the same star schema but stores it in PostgreSQL, a row-oriented
relational database. It offers full support for the Kimball design---enforced primary and
foreign keys, transactions, and in-place updates---but reads entire rows even when a query
needs only a few columns, which is expected to penalize the large scans of BR1--BR10.

\subsection{Additional Alternatives}
\label{sec:sol-additional}

\subsubsection{In-Database Streaming for BR7}
BR7 is also implemented with a ClickHouse Kafka engine table and a materialized view that
applies the same anomaly rules inside the database, instead of Spark Structured Streaming.
This removes one component but limits the logic to what SQL can express.

\subsubsection{Lakehouse (Optional)}
If time permits, the star schema is also stored as Delta Lake tables and queried with Spark
SQL, representing the lakehouse paradigm \cite{armbrust2021lakehouse}.

\subsection{Related Work}
Kimball's bottom-up dimensional modeling \cite{kimball2013} contrasts with Inmon's top-down
approach \cite{inmon2005}, in which a normalized enterprise data warehouse feeds
departmental marts, and with Data Vault \cite{linstedt2015}, which separates business keys,
relationships, and descriptive attributes to favor auditability and change. For a single
business process and a small team, Kimball offers the shortest path from data to
user-facing queries. The lakehouse paradigm \cite{armbrust2021lakehouse} unifies data lakes
and warehouses on open formats. The advantages of column stores for analytical workloads
are well established \cite{stonebraker2005cstore,abadi2008column}, and the Star Schema
Benchmark \cite{oneil2009ssb} adapts TPC-H to star schemas; our assessment follows the same
idea of measuring star-join queries at increasing scale factors.

Performance is assessed by four metrics: (i) query latency of each BR query (median and

(iii) load and ETL time; and (iv) for BR7, end-to-end latency and throughput in events per

every scale factor without violating Criterion 1.

Effectiveness is assessed per BR by (i) whether the BR is demonstrated successfully end to
end through the application, (ii) the number of user interactions needed to obtain the

System Usability Scale (SUS) \cite{brooke1996sus}, where a score above 68 is considered
above average.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{H1.} The One Big Table answers queries slightly faster than the star schema,
        but the star schema needs less storage and updates a customer attribute by
        rewriting one dimension row instead of many fact rows.
  \item \textbf{H2.} ClickHouse outperforms PostgreSQL on every BR, with a gap that widens
        as the scale factor grows.
  \item \textbf{H3.} The ClickHouse Kafka engine yields lower latency for simple rules,
        while Spark Structured Streaming offers more flexibility.
  \item \textbf{H4.} Spark ETL time grows roughly linearly with data size and decreases as
        more cores are used.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Threats to Validity}
The results should be interpreted with caution. The data is synthetic, so behavioral
findings may not transfer to a real bank. Scaling by replication preserves the
distribution of the original data and may therefore favor compression more than real data
would. All measurements are taken on a single machine, so they do not capture the network
effects of a distributed deployment. Two properties of the synthetic data also limit
behavioral conclusions: coverage is effectively limited to 2016-08-01--2016-09-21, and many
customers share identical balances to the last rupee (e.g., 115,035,495.10~INR), which
suggests generated rather than observed values.

\section{Conclusions and Recommendations}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This report presented a Kimball dimensional data warehouse for banking analytics built with
Kafka, Spark, and ClickHouse. Profiling showed that the source, although advertised as
analytics-ready, has serious quality defects; the proposed solution resolves them through a
redefined customer identity, explicit cleaning rules with an audit trail, and marts rebuilt
from the atomic fact table. Ten business requirements for seven user groups are served
through dashboards and a near-real-time alert page.

\textbf{Limitations.} The data covers only 55 days, of which only two fall in October; most customers have a single
transaction; transaction types and several customer attributes are rule-inferred or
simulated; there is no time of day; and the absence of effective dates prevents a genuine
slowly changing dimension.

\textbf{Future work.} Extend the model with additional business processes (e.g., account
opening and loans) sharing conformed dimensions through a bus matrix; demonstrate SCD
Type 2 on attributes with real change history; replace rule-based alerts with learned
anomaly detection; and evaluate a distributed deployment.
